perf(P136): the 31x gap was a measurement-basis mismatch; add a sync-basis probe

fhz ran section 7 on Ascend. Everything is fast: baseline gather 0.020 ms,
index_select 0.026, take 0.011, the official gather operator 0.033 (still slower
than the baseline it would replace), einsum forward 0.155, the addmm_ update
(which touches roughly 100 MiB) only 0.060, cross-entropy 0.116, and the
per-step host-side np.array_equal 0.007.

Items 1 through 7 total about 0.35 ms, yet the training log reports the readout
at 10.99 ms - a 31x gap. That gap is the whole story, and I now think I know
what it is.

Every number above is a SUBMISSION measurement. The NPU executes
asynchronously, so when you run best-of-N back to back, the previous result is
still sitting in the queue and what you are timing is the cost of enqueueing
work, not the cost of finishing it. The training log's "readout ms/tok" is a
main-thread wall clock from time.perf_counter (model.py:370-410), which
includes waiting for the H2D copy and every host-side cost, so it corresponds
to the SYNCHRONOUS basis. The two differ by 31x precisely because one measures
enqueueing and the other measures completion.

New section 8 measures this directly: it builds a production AccelReadout,
calls forward_dev plus learn_softmax, and times both the bare call and the same
call followed by a device synchronize. 8b is the number that reconciles with the
training log.

A latent trap found while writing it: forward_dev only exists on the accelerated
backend, while the numba fallback arm has just __call__ plus learn. My first
version only implemented the device path, so on this host - which takes the
fallback - section 8 would never run, while on Ascend it would. "Verified
locally" would have been a fiction. Both branches are now handled and the tool
prints which interface it took.

Direction depends on what 8b reports. If it is around 10 ms, the cost is real
device execution or waiting, and the levers are fewer kernels per step
(torch.compile fusion, already unblocked in P128 but off by default) and less
traffic. If it is far below 10 ms, then the 10 ms is host-side: the per-step
array_equal in _lookup_ht, the h assembly in _build_h, the pinned-buffer
rotation, in which case the answer is to cut host work rather than touch NPU
kernels. Note that the update is NOT the culprit at 0.060 ms, which rules out
one of my earlier hypotheses.
